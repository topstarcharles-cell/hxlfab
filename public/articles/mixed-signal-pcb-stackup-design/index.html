Mixed-Signal PCB Stackup Design: How to Keep Analog and Digital Quiet on One Board

Mixed-signal PCB stackup design: analog/digital partitioning, ground plane strategy, return paths and layer planning to keep noise out of sensitive circuits.

Published November 12, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

A mixed-signal board carries two kinds of circuits that do not get along. On one side, a microcontroller or FPGA switches tens of millions of times per second, pulling sharp current spikes from its supply. On the other side, an ADC, a sensor front end or an audio amplifier measures signals in millivolts — sometimes microvolts. Put them on the same board without a plan, and the digital side will shout straight into the analog side's ear.

The stackup is where that plan starts. Layer count, layer order, and where the ground planes sit decide how much noise couples from one domain to the other before a single trace is routed. Get the stackup right and the layout becomes manageable; get it wrong and no amount of careful routing fully recovers the analog performance.

Partition first, then stack

Before choosing layers, draw the floorplan. Place all analog circuitry in one region and all digital circuitry in another, with a clear boundary between them. Converters — ADCs and DACs — sit on the boundary, because they belong to both worlds: their digital pins face the digital side, their analog pins face the analog side.

This partition drives every later decision. Traces should not cross the boundary except at the converter and at deliberate, controlled points, and power should enter each region separately.

Split ground vs solid ground

The classic debate in mixed-signal design is whether to split the ground plane into separate analog and digital regions, joined at a single point, or to keep one solid plane under the whole board.

A split ground with a single-point connection — a narrow bridge, often placed under the converter — forces digital return currents to stay on the digital side, because the only path between the regions is the bridge. It works well when the analog signals are very quiet and the digital currents are large, and it is the approach most textbooks recommend.

But a split is easy to get wrong. Any trace that crosses the split without a nearby bridge forces its return current to detour around the gap, creating a large loop that radiates and couples noise — often worse than no split at all. Every signal crossing the boundary needs its crossing point planned, and fast digital signals should never cross a split.

A solid ground plane avoids that failure mode entirely: return currents take the shortest path, directly under their traces, and nothing can accidentally create a slot. For many boards, especially with moderate analog sensitivity, a solid plane plus careful partitioning and routing is the safer choice. The digital noise still shares the plane, but a solid plane's impedance is low, and distance plus layout discipline does most of the isolation work.

The practical rule: use a split only when you can guarantee no trace crosses it except at the bridge, and when the analog section is sensitive enough to justify the discipline. Otherwise, keep the plane solid, partition the placement strictly, and let distance do the work.

Planning the layers

A four-layer board is the minimum that does mixed-signal work well: top signal, ground, power, bottom signal is the common arrangement, with the ground plane adjacent to the top layer so that high-speed traces have an immediate reference. The analog and digital sections share the plane, so partitioning and routing discipline carry the isolation.

A six-layer board gives the stackup real room to breathe: signal, ground, signal, power, ground, signal. The two ground planes shield the inner signal layer, the analog signals can be routed on the inner layer sandwiched between grounds, and the power plane can be split into separate analog and digital regions. With support for up to 30 layers and finished thicknesses from 0.15 to 7.0 mm, HXLFAB can build these stackups in standard FR-4, with TG135, TG150 or TG170 laminate grades chosen to match the board's thermal and reliability requirements.

On eight layers and above, the usual approach is to dedicate one or two inner layers entirely to quiet analog routing, shielded on both sides by ground planes, while the digital signals live on the outer and remaining inner layers. The extra layers also let you give the analog section its own power plane region, physically separated from the digital power by a gap in the copper.

Return paths: the detail that decides everything

Every signal current returns to its source, and it returns through the nearest plane, directly under the trace, as long as that plane is continuous. Break the plane — with a split, a slot, or a dense field of vias — and the return current detours, the loop area grows, and the trace starts radiating and picking up noise.

Three rules follow. Route high-speed digital traces over an unbroken ground plane, and never route them across a split or a moat. Keep vias out of the analog ground region where possible: each via punches a hole in the plane, and a cluster of them under an ADC raises the local impedance. And when a signal must cross between regions, cross it at the single-point bridge, where the return current has a defined path — never at a random point along the split.

Microvias help here: a 0.10 mm laser via takes far less plane area than a mechanical drill, so fan-out under dense converters and BGAs can be done without shredding the reference plane. For boards built to 3/3 mil trace and space rules, small vias and tight fan-out are what make a dense mixed-signal layout possible at all.

Power: separate rails, separate decoupling

Digital switching noise rides power rails. If the analog section shares the digital supply rail, that noise feeds straight into amplifiers and references. Give the analog section its own regulator or its own filtered branch — a ferrite bead and local decoupling capacitors between the digital supply and the analog rail is the standard approach — and keep the analog power plane region separate from the digital one. Place small decoupling capacitors close to each analog IC's supply pins, and never share a decoupling capacitor between the two domains: it is a shared noise path.

Reducing crosstalk in the layout

With the stackup and floorplan set, crosstalk comes down to spacing and routing. Keep fast digital traces away from analog traces — a good starting rule is at least three times the trace width of separation, more for the quietest analog lines. Never run a digital trace parallel to an analog trace for a long distance; if they must cross, cross at right angles, which minimizes the coupled length. Use guard traces — grounded traces run alongside a sensitive analog line — where spacing alone is not enough, and tie the guard to ground at frequent intervals so it actually shields rather than floating.

On multilayer boards, the stackup itself is a crosstalk tool: routing the quietest analog signals on an inner layer between two ground planes shields them from digital traces on the outer layers. And remember that the analog section's worst enemy is often a digital trace on the layer directly above or below it, coupled through the thin dielectric — which is why adjacent signal layers without a plane between them are a stackup error, not just a routing inconvenience.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is splitting the ground plane and then routing traces across the split. The split only works if nothing crosses it except at the bridge; a single high-speed trace crossing elsewhere turns the split into a radiator. If your layout cannot guarantee that, do not split.

A second mistake is placing the converter in the wrong spot. An ADC with its digital pins facing the analog section forces digital traces through analog territory. Put the converter on the boundary, digital side facing digital, analog side facing analog.

A third is sharing power and decoupling between domains. One regulator feeding both sides, or one decoupling capacitor serving both an analog and a digital IC, couples the two domains through the supply no matter how good the ground strategy is.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Analog and digital sections partitioned, with the converter on the boundary
  • [ ] Ground strategy chosen — split with a single-point bridge, or solid plane with strict partitioning — and every trace crossing checked against it
  • [ ] Every signal layer adjacent to a plane; no two signal layers stacked without a plane between them
  • [ ] Ground plane continuous under the analog section, with via fields kept out of it
  • [ ] High-speed digital traces routed over unbroken ground, never across a split
  • [ ] Analog signals given separation from digital traces, right-angle crossings, and shielded inner-layer routing where needed
  • [ ] Separate analog power rail or filtered branch, with local decoupling per analog IC
  • [ ] Return paths verified: no trace crosses a ground split except at the bridge
  • [ ] Stackup, laminate grade and layer count confirmed with the fabricator before ordering
