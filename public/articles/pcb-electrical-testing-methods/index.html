PCB Electrical Testing Methods: Flying Probe vs Fixture and How to Specify the Right Test

Flying probe or bed-of-nails fixture? Learn how PCB electrical testing works, what each method catches, and how to design test points for reliable boards.

Published November 9, 2026

An optical inspection can see a solder bridge. It cannot see a hairline crack inside a plated hole, a via that never connected to its inner layer, or a short buried between two internal planes. That is the job of electrical testing: every net on the board is checked for the two failures that matter — opens, where a connection that should exist does not, and shorts, where a connection that should not exist does.

Electrical testing is the last gate before a board ships. It does not improve a design; it proves the fabricated board matches the design. This guide explains the two main methods, what each one catches, and how to design and specify a test so it actually protects your build.

What an electrical test checks

At its simplest, an electrical test compares the board against the netlist — the list of connections taken from the design data. The tester applies a small voltage or current to one net at a time and measures resistance to every other net. A net that should be connected but shows high resistance is an open. Two nets that should be isolated but show low resistance are a short.

Two parameters define how strict the test is. The continuity threshold is the maximum resistance a good connection may show, typically in the low ohms; anything above it is flagged as an open or a marginal connection. The isolation threshold is the minimum resistance between nets that must stay separate, typically in the megohms; anything below it is flagged as a short or leakage. Tighter thresholds catch more defects but also raise the false-failure rate on long or thin traces, so the values should suit the board — a dense board with 3/3 mil trace and space needs isolation limits chosen with its fine spacing in mind.

A test also needs access. The tester can only probe what it can touch, so every net must reach a testable point: a component pad, a via, or a dedicated test pad. Nets with no accessible point are untestable, and the test report should say so rather than silently passing them. That coverage figure — tested nets as a percentage of total nets — is the single most useful number on a test report.

Flying probe: flexible, slower, no fixture

A flying probe tester moves a set of probes, usually four to eight, across the board under program control, touching test points one net at a time. It reads the netlist directly from the design data, so there is no fixture to build and no tooling charge. That makes it the natural choice for prototypes and small batches, where a fixture would cost more than the boards.

Its limits are speed and force. Because probes move sequentially, a board with thousands of nets takes minutes to test, so flying probe does not scale to volume production. Probes also make small, soft contact, which is fine for continuity but less reliable for very low resistance measurements. And a flying probe can only test what its probes can reach — test points buried under components or hidden on the underside of a dense assembly are inaccessible without a fixture or a second pass.

Bed of nails: fast, complete, needs a fixture

A bed-of-nails fixture is a plate of spring-loaded pins arranged to touch every test point on the board at once. The board is pressed onto the fixture, all nets are tested in parallel, and a full test takes seconds. That speed is why fixtures dominate volume production.

The trade-off is upfront cost and lead time: the fixture must be designed and built for one specific board, and any board revision that moves a test point can require a fixture change. Fixtures also need the board to be designed for them — test points on a regular grid, kept clear of tall components, with enough clearance around each point for the pin and its spring. A fixture pin pressing on a via that is also a component pad can damage the pad, so test points should be dedicated pads where possible, not shared with solder joints.

The economics are simple: divide the fixture cost by the number of boards it will test. At a few dozen boards, flying probe wins; at a few thousand, the fixture pays for itself many times over.

In-circuit test and functional test: beyond opens and shorts

Electrical testing answers one question: is the copper connected correctly? Two further test stages answer different questions and are often confused with it.

In-circuit test, or ICT, also uses a bed-of-nails fixture, but instead of only checking continuity it powers the assembled board and measures components in place — resistor values, capacitor presence, diode orientation, and simple IC behaviour. ICT catches assembly errors that a bare-board test cannot, such as a wrong-value resistor or a reversed diode. It needs test access to component nodes, so it must be designed in from the start, and its fixture costs more than a bare-board fixture.

Functional test goes further still: the assembled board is powered and run through its real operating behaviour — inputs are applied, outputs are measured, and the board must perform. It catches firmware and interaction faults that no component measurement finds, but it needs test software and a test jig, and someone to maintain both. For most boards, the practical stack is bare-board electrical test at fabrication, then ICT or functional test at assembly, each catching what the previous stage cannot.

Designing for testability

Most test failures are designed in, not manufactured in. A few rules keep test coverage high and test cost low.

First, give every net a test point. A net that ends only at a BGA ball or an inner-layer via with no exposed pad cannot be tested — add a small dedicated test pad for such nets, ideally on the bottom side where a fixture can reach it. Second, keep test points on a sensible grid and clear of components: a point under a tall capacitor or next to a connector shell is unreachable. Third, size the points for the method and keep clearance from nearby traces so the probe does not bridge to a neighbour. Fourth, on dense boards with 0.10 mm laser-drilled microvias, confirm with the fabricator which vias are testable: a microvia buried under a pad may not be reachable from the surface.

Finally, include a test coupon when the design needs more than continuity. Impedance-controlled boards are verified by measuring a test coupon with a time-domain reflectometer rather than by probing the board itself. State the coupon requirement and the target impedance in the fabrication notes, and agree the acceptance limits before the run starts.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is assuming a board was tested because it was inspected. Automated optical inspection checks the surface; it cannot check inner-layer connections, and on a multilayer board — HXLFAB builds up to 30 layers — most of the copper is invisible to a camera. Always ask for the electrical test report, not just an inspection result.

A second mistake is specifying "100% electrical test" without defining coverage. A test that touches 85% of nets and passes the rest untested is not a 100% test. Ask for the coverage figure and the list of untested nets, then decide whether the untested nets are acceptable — a power plane with many parallel vias may be fine untested, a single signal net is not.

A third is designing test points after the layout is finished. Plan test access when the layout starts, alongside component placement, not as an afterthought.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Electrical test specified in the fabrication notes (flying probe or fixture, with the method stated)
  • [ ] Continuity and isolation thresholds agreed with the fabricator, suited to the board's trace and space
  • [ ] Every net has an accessible test point; untested nets identified and accepted
  • [ ] Test points on a workable grid, clear of tall components and connector shells
  • [ ] Test pad size and clearance suited to the chosen method
  • [ ] Microvia and buried-via testability confirmed with the fabricator
  • [ ] Test coverage figure requested on the report, not just a pass/fail
  • [ ] Impedance coupon specified where controlled impedance is required
  • [ ] Fixture economics checked for production volumes before committing to flying probe
