PCB Solder Mask and Silkscreen: How to Specify the Two Layers That Protect and Label Your Board

Solder mask and silkscreen explained: LPI vs dry film, mask dam and clearance rules, colour choice, and silkscreen tips for readable, solderable boards.

Published November 11, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

A finished circuit board is mostly green, black or white — and that colour is not decoration. It is the solder mask, a thin polymer coating that covers the copper, leaves only the pads and test points exposed, and decides where solder can and cannot flow. On top of it sits the silkscreen, the printed text and symbols that tell the assembler which component goes where.

Both layers are specified in the same Gerber package as the copper, and both are easy to get wrong: a mask opening a fraction too small starves a joint of solder, a mask dam too thin disappears during processing, and silkscreen printed over a pad contaminates the joint.

What solder mask does

Solder mask has three jobs. It insulates: bare copper oxidizes and can short to neighbouring traces, so the mask seals it against air, moisture and handling. It controls solder: during reflow, molten solder only wets the exposed pads, while the mask keeps solder from bridging adjacent pads or wicking down a trace. And it protects thin traces against scratches during handling and assembly.

The mask goes on after etching and plating, before the surface finish; its openings are defined by the solder mask Gerber layer.

LPI vs dry film

Most boards today use liquid photoimageable solder mask (LPI): applied as a liquid, dried, exposed through a photographic image of the mask pattern, and developed to wash away the unexposed areas. LPI follows the surface topography well and gives good resolution on fine features, making it the standard choice for most boards, including high-density designs.

Dry film solder mask is applied as a laminated sheet, then exposed and developed the same way. It gives a very uniform thickness, but bridges less well over uneven topography and is less common on fine-pitch work. For most designs the fabricator defaults to LPI.

Solder mask defined vs non-solder-mask defined pads

Every pad is either solder-mask defined (SMD) or non-solder-mask defined (NSMD), and the distinction matters for soldering and mechanical strength.

With an NSMD pad, the mask opening is larger than the copper pad, so the whole pad is exposed with a ring of bare laminate around it and the solder joint wets the full pad and its sides, giving a strong joint. NSMD is the standard choice for most surface-mount components, including fine-pitch QFN and BGA packages, where the opening must be larger than the pad to avoid encroaching on the solderable area.

With an SMD pad, the mask opening is smaller than the copper pad, so the mask overlaps the pad edge and defines the solderable area. The exposed copper is smaller and precisely bounded, which helps control solder volume where too much solder causes bridging — for example, some connectors and larger-pitch components. The trade-off is that solder cannot wet the pad edges, so the joint is slightly weaker. Use NSMD for fine-pitch and area-array parts where joint strength matters, and consider SMD where solder volume control is the priority. Flag the choice in your fabrication notes so the manufacturer does not silently convert one to the other.

Solder mask dams and clearance

Between two adjacent pads, a strip of mask — the dam — keeps solder from bridging the gap. The dam is only as good as the fabricator can resolve it: if the gap is too small for a dam to survive exposure and development, the mask between the pads washes away, the two pads share one opening, and bridging becomes likely.

This is a real constraint on fine-pitch designs: a board built to 3/3 mil trace and space rules, as HXLFAB supports, can have pad gaps narrow enough that a dam is marginal or impossible. At that pitch, the solder paste stencil and the reflow profile control bridging rather than the mask, so tell the fabricator the pitch and let them advise whether a dam is achievable or the pads need a shared opening with a modified stencil.

Two practical rules follow. First, never assume a dam exists just because you drew one: if the gap is below the fabricator's minimum dam width, it will not survive. Second, keep mask openings clear of vias that are not meant to be soldered. A via inside a pad, or too close to one, can wick solder away from the joint through the hole — a problem known as solder thief. Tent vias with mask, or plug them, when they sit near pads that need their full solder volume.

Choosing a solder mask colour

Green is the default for good reasons: it is the most mature process, it has the best contrast for automated optical inspection, and it is the cheapest. But colour is a real design choice, not just a branding one.

Black looks premium and hides the copper, but it absorbs heat and reduces contrast for inspection — for machines and for a technician reading the board. White reflects heat and gives strong contrast for black silkscreen, which is why it is popular on LED boards, but it shows dirt and can yellow with heat. Red and blue sit in the middle: good contrast, distinctive appearance, widely available.

If the board will be inspected optically or reworked by hand, favour a colour with good contrast against the copper and the silkscreen; dark masks also absorb more radiant heat than light ones.

Silkscreen: how to keep it readable

Silkscreen is the printed ink layer on top of the mask, carrying reference designators, polarity marks, logos and assembly notes for the assembler and the repair technician.

The most common silkscreen failure is ink printed where solder needs to go. Silkscreen must never overlap a pad, a test point or a mask opening: ink on a pad contaminates the joint, and ink under a component can lift it or interfere with reflow. Keep a clear gap between silkscreen and any solderable feature, and check the silkscreen Gerber against the mask Gerber before release — not just against the copper.

The second failure is unreadable text. Silkscreen has a minimum legible height and stroke width, and text smaller than that blurs into a smudge after printing. As a general rule, keep reference designators at least 1 mm high with a stroke width of at least 0.15 mm, and larger where space allows. Text that is too small is worse than no text: it invites misreading. If a designator cannot fit at a readable size, move it outside the component outline with a leader line, or place it on the opposite side of the board.

The third failure is text hidden after assembly. A reference designator printed under a component is invisible once the component is placed. Place designators adjacent to the component, not underneath it, and put polarity marks and pin-1 indicators where they remain visible after soldering — a small dot or notch at the corner of the footprint, outside the body outline. Finally, silkscreen is printed last and has the loosest registration of any layer, so keep text away from board edges and keep-outs so a small shift does not push it onto a pad.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is drawing mask openings the same size as the pads. Mask registration has a tolerance, and an opening drawn exactly to the pad edge will encroach on the pad on one side and expose extra laminate on the other. Give openings a deliberate clearance over the pad for NSMD designs, or a deliberate overlap for SMD designs — never a zero nominal.

A second mistake is forgetting that vias need mask too. An untented via near a pad wicks solder away from the joint; an untented via under a BGA can trap air and flux. Tent or plug vias that sit in or near solderable areas.

A third is silkscreen over pads. It looks harmless on screen because the silkscreen layer is drawn on top, but ink on a pad is a soldering defect. Always run a design-rule check that flags silkscreen overlapping mask openings.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Mask openings defined as NSMD or SMD per component type, and the choice noted in fabrication notes
  • [ ] Mask dam width checked against the fabricator's minimum for the tightest pad pitch
  • [ ] Vias near pads tented or plugged to prevent solder wicking
  • [ ] Mask colour chosen deliberately, with inspection contrast and heat considered
  • [ ] Silkscreen clear of all pads, test points and mask openings
  • [ ] Reference designators at least 1 mm high, placed adjacent to components, not underneath them
  • [ ] Polarity and pin-1 marks visible after assembly
  • [ ] Silkscreen kept away from board edges
