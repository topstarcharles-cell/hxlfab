PCB Surface Finish Selection: A Practical Guide to HASL, ENIG, OSP and More

HASL, ENIG, OSP, immersion tin, immersion silver or hard gold? Compare shelf life, planarity, cost and assembly fit in this practical PCB surface finish guide.

Published November 8, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

A surface finish is the thin coating on a printed circuit board's exposed copper. It keeps the copper from oxidising before assembly and gives solder something it can wet to during assembly. The choice looks minor — one line in the fabrication notes — but it affects solderability, shelf life, the flatness of fine-pitch pads, and cost. A wrong choice can mean bad joints, a failed inspection, or boards that go out of date on the shelf before assembly.

HXLFAB offers HASL, ENIG, OSP, immersion tin, immersion silver and hard gold, subject to specification. This guide explains what each finish does well, where it struggles, and how to choose.

What a surface finish has to do

Bare copper oxidises in air, and oxidised pads do not wet: solder balls up instead of flowing, joints go dull and grainy, and in the worst case a component does not connect at all. A good finish must protect the copper during storage, wet evenly when solder arrives, and stay flat enough that fine-pitch parts sit level.

Planarity matters most for fine-pitch BGAs and QFNs — a domed pad can tilt a part or leave an open joint. Contact behaviour matters wherever the board is not soldered at all: edge connectors, keypads, and test points need a finish that survives repeated contact without wearing through.

HASL: the workhorse

Hot air solder levelling coats exposed copper with solder — tin-lead or lead-free — and levels it with a blast of hot air. It is the oldest finish in volume production: the pad is already solder, so it wets excellently, and it is inexpensive. For simple boards, through-hole designs, and hand assembly, HASL is hard to beat on value.

Its weakness is planarity. Hot air does not level perfectly, so pads come out slightly domed, thicker in the middle than at the edges. On coarse-pitch parts that does not matter, but on a fine-pitch BGA or a 0.4 mm pitch QFN an uneven pad can mean a tilted part or an open joint, and HASL can bridge very tight pad spacings. Rule of thumb: if the finest pitch is 0.5 mm or above and there are no BGAs, HASL is a safe, economical choice; below that, pick a flatter finish.

ENIG: the flat, versatile default

Electroless nickel immersion gold deposits nickel over the copper, then a very thin layer of gold over the nickel. The nickel stops the copper oxidising and stops solder dissolving the copper; the gold only protects the nickel until soldering, when it dissolves into the joint. ENIG's strength is flatness — chemical deposition follows the copper surface instead of building a dome — so it suits fine-pitch BGAs, QFNs, and any design where pad planarity matters.

It also stores well, typically around twelve months, and it solders, wire-bonds, and makes a usable contact surface for probing. Its drawbacks are cost — one of the more expensive finishes — and a known failure mode called black pad, where the nickel corrodes during gold deposition and the joint later fractures. Black pad is rare with a controlled process, so ENIG should come from a fabricator with a controlled bath, not the cheapest quote.

OSP: the flat, low-cost option

Organic solderability preservative is a thin organic coating applied directly to the copper. It is flat, inexpensive, and works well for single reflow assembly — for a straightforward SMT board that will be assembled promptly, OSP is often the best value of all the finishes.

Its limit is shelf life and rework tolerance. The coating is thin and degrades with heat: each reflow cycle thins it further, and after two or three reflows a board may no longer wet reliably. Typical shelf life is around six months, and handling matters — fingerprints and scratches damage the coating directly. OSP suits boards that go from fabrication to assembly quickly; it is a poor choice for a board that will sit in a warehouse or need repeated rework.

Immersion tin and immersion silver

Immersion tin deposits a thin layer of tin directly on the copper. It is flat, solders well, and works with press-fit connectors. Its drawbacks are shelf life — typically around six months — and a tendency for tin whiskers to grow over long storage, which can bridge fine spacings.

Immersion silver deposits a thin silver layer, also flat, with excellent solderability and good performance at high frequencies, where silver's conductivity helps as skin effect matters. Its weaknesses are tarnish and cost: silver tarnishes in air, especially in the presence of sulphur, so shelf life is typically six to twelve months. Either immersion finish is a reasonable middle ground where ENIG's cost is hard to justify, provided the board is assembled within its storage window.

Hard gold: for contacts, not for soldering

Hard gold — electrolytic gold over nickel, much thicker than ENIG's immersion gold — is the finish for edge connectors, gold fingers, and keypads: hard, wear-resistant, and able to survive thousands of insertion cycles. That is also why it should not be used as a general solder finish. Thick gold dissolves into solder and embrittles the joint, so solder pads finished in hard gold can crack under stress. Use hard gold only where the board makes mechanical contact, and a different finish — usually ENIG — on the solder pads. When a design needs both, the fabricator applies them selectively, so state the gold-finger areas clearly on the fabrication drawing.

How to choose

Start from the assembly, not the price list. Four questions settle most decisions. First, the finest pitch: at 0.5 mm and below, or with any BGA, choose a flat finish — ENIG, OSP, immersion tin, or immersion silver. Second, storage time and heat cycles: long storage or multiple reflows point to ENIG, a quick single-pass build suits OSP, and HASL tolerates storage and rework well. Third, contacts, edge fingers, or wire bonding: fingers need hard gold, wire bonding usually needs ENIG, and a board with both needs a selective finish callout. Fourth, high-frequency or RF sections: immersion silver and ENIG are the usual candidates, while HASL's uneven solder can disturb controlled impedance. Cost is the last filter: OSP and HASL are the economical options, immersion tin and silver sit in the middle, ENIG is the premium all-rounder, and hard gold is priced by area, so keep gold-finger regions small.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is leaving the finish off the fabrication notes entirely. The fabricator then builds to its default, which may not suit the design — a fine-pitch BGA board built in HASL is a classic example. Always state the finish explicitly, including lead-free or tin-lead for HASL. A second mistake is mixing finishes without saying so: a board that needs hard gold fingers and ENIG pads must say which areas get which finish, so mark the finger areas on the fabrication drawing. A third is ignoring storage. OSP and immersion finishes have real shelf lives; ordering boards months ahead of assembly without checking the finish's storage window is how boards arrive oxidised or tarnished. If assembly will be delayed, ENIG or HASL is the safer choice.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Surface finish stated in the fabrication notes (HASL, ENIG, OSP, immersion tin, immersion silver, or hard gold)
  • [ ] Flat finish (ENIG, OSP, immersion) chosen for 0.5 mm pitch and below or any BGA
  • [ ] HASL specified as lead-free or tin-lead where it is used
  • [ ] Hard gold limited to contact and finger areas, marked on the fabrication drawing
  • [ ] Shelf life checked against the assembly schedule (about 6 months for OSP and immersion tin, 6–12 months for immersion silver, about 12 months for ENIG)
  • [ ] Reflow cycles planned and compatible with the finish
  • [ ] Wire-bonding or contact requirements stated, with the matching finish specified
  • [ ] Finish reviewed together with stackup and impedance requirements where RF sections exist
