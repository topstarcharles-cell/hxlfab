FR-4 Tg Grades Compared: TG135 vs TG150 vs TG170 and How to Choose

TG135, TG150 and TG170 compared: what glass transition temperature means for your PCB, when each grade is enough, and when paying for higher Tg is worth it.

Published November 16, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

Every FR-4 datasheet carries a Tg number — TG135, TG150, TG170 are the three grades most designers meet. The number looks small and technical, but it decides whether a board survives lead-free assembly, how many rework cycles it tolerates, and whether a dense multilayer board stays flat through production. Picking the wrong grade is one of the cheapest mistakes a design can make, and one of the most expensive to fix after the boards are built.

What Tg actually is

Tg, the glass transition temperature, is the temperature at which the epoxy resin in the laminate changes from a hard, glassy state to a softer, rubbery one. Below Tg the resin is rigid and the board holds its shape; above Tg the resin softens, and the laminate's coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) rises sharply — especially in the Z-axis, the direction through the board thickness.

That Z-axis expansion is the practical problem. When resin expands much faster than the copper plating in a via barrel, the plating is stretched. Repeat that through several reflow cycles and the plating cracks, the via goes open, and the board fails intermittently — the worst kind of failure to chase down.

Tg is not a melting point. FR-4 does not melt at Tg; it softens. It is also not the whole thermal story — the decomposition temperature (Td) and the time-to-delamination (T260/T288) matter too — but Tg is the single number most fabrication drawings specify, and the easiest grade choice to get right.

The three grades at a glance

| Grade | Tg | Typical character | |---|---|---| | TG135 | ~135 °C | Standard FR-4. Fine for simple, low-heat boards. | | TG150 | ~150 °C | Mid Tg. The workhorse for most commercial multilayer boards. | | TG170 | ~170 °C | High Tg. For lead-free assembly, thick multilayers and hot environments. |

The step from one grade to the next is not just a number on a datasheet: higher-Tg resins are formulated differently, with a higher cross-link density that keeps the laminate stiffer at elevated temperature and reduces Z-axis expansion above Tg. In practice, the higher the grade, the more thermal excursions the board tolerates before the vias and the laminate start to suffer.

Why lead-free assembly changed the choice

The question that decides the grade is usually simple: how hot does the board get, and how often?

Lead-free soldering changed the answer for almost everyone. A lead-free reflow profile peaks around 245–260 °C, well above the Tg of every grade — but the board only spends a short time at peak, and what matters is how the laminate behaves during that excursion. A TG135 board taken through two or three lead-free reflow passes plus a wave or selective solder step is operating with little thermal margin; a TG170 board has far more headroom for the same process.

Rules of thumb that hold up in practice:

  • TG135 is adequate for single- and double-sided boards, low layer counts, and designs assembled with a single low-temperature process — or where the board never sees reflow at all.
  • TG150 covers the middle ground: 4–8 layer boards, standard lead-free reflow, and most commercial and consumer products. For many designs it is the safe default.
  • TG170 is the choice when the board is thick, has many layers, goes through multiple reflow cycles or rework, runs hot in service, or carries fine-pitch BGAs where via reliability is non-negotiable.

HXLFAB builds with all three grades — TG135, TG150 and TG170 — so the grade is a design decision, not a supply constraint. The material is available in 1–30 layer stackups with finished thickness from 0.15 to 7.0 mm, so the same grade choice applies from a simple two-layer board to a thick multilayer.

What higher Tg buys you — and what it costs

Reliability through assembly. The clearest payoff is via reliability. Every reflow cycle, every rework pass, every repair heats the board past Tg. Higher Tg means less Z-axis expansion per cycle and less accumulated strain on plated barrels — particularly important for thick boards, where the expansion is multiplied by the thickness, and for blind and buried via structures that cannot be inspected from the surface.

Dimensional stability in multilayer lamination. Thick multilayer boards are laminated in several press cycles. Higher-Tg materials hold their dimensions better through repeated pressing, which matters for registration on dense designs with 3/3 mil trace and space and 0.10 mm laser-drilled microvias.

Stability at operating temperature. Boards that run hot in service — power electronics, LED drivers, boards mounted near heat sources — benefit from a resin that stays glassy well above the operating temperature. A board running at 110 °C on TG135 is only 25 °C below its Tg; on TG170 it has 60 °C of margin.

The cost. Higher-Tg laminate costs more per panel, drills and processes slightly differently, and can be a little more brittle. On a simple board the premium buys nothing measurable — paying for TG170 on a two-layer board that sees one reflow pass is wasted money. The right grade is the lowest one that survives the board's actual thermal history with margin, not the highest one in the catalogue.

How Tg interacts with the rest of the stackup

Tg does not act alone. Three interactions catch designers out:

Layer count and thickness. A 20-layer, 3 mm board sees far more thermal stress in reflow than a 4-layer, 1.6 mm board. The thicker the board and the more layers, the stronger the case for a higher grade.

Copper weight. Heavy copper — up to 4 oz inner and 8 oz outer — adds thermal mass and changes how the board heats and cools. It also makes drilling and plating more demanding, and the laminate must survive the extra thermal load of processing thick copper. High-Tg material pairs naturally with heavy-copper designs.

High-frequency laminates. PTFE-based materials used for RF work have very different thermal behaviour from FR-4. In hybrid stackups that mix PTFE and FR-4, the FR-4 layers should be chosen with the same thermal discipline — a TG170 FR-4 core in a hybrid build is often the right call, since the PTFE layers already demand careful processing.

Common mistakes

Specifying TG135 by default on a multilayer lead-free board. The default on many old templates is standard Tg. If the board is 8 layers or more and goes through lead-free reflow, that default is a risk — check it before the design is frozen.

Specifying TG170 everywhere "to be safe". Over-specification wastes money and can complicate processing. A simple board does not need high Tg, and a fabricator quoting TG170 on a two-layer design should be asked why.

Forgetting rework and repair. The assembly plan may call for one reflow pass, but a board that needs a BGA replaced sees a second localised heat cycle, and a returned unit may see a third. If rework is likely, allow for it in the grade choice.

Writing "high Tg" without a number. "High Tg" means different things to different suppliers. Put the number on the drawing — TG135, TG150 or TG170 — so there is no ambiguity at quoting or at incoming inspection.

Ignoring the rest of the thermal datasheet. Tg is the headline number, but Td (decomposition temperature) and T260/T288 (time to delamination) tell you how the material actually behaves at soldering temperatures. Ask for the full datasheet when the thermal budget is tight.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Assembly process confirmed: lead-free reflow peaks and number of passes
  • [ ] Tg grade specified by number (TG135 / TG150 / TG170) on the fabrication drawing
  • [ ] Layer count and finished thickness confirmed (1–30 layers; 0.15–7.0 mm)
  • [ ] Rework and repair cycles allowed for in the grade choice
  • [ ] Operating temperature checked against the grade's margin above Tg
  • [ ] Heavy copper requirements noted (up to 4 oz inner / 8 oz outer)
  • [ ] Hybrid PTFE/FR-4 stackup reviewed, if applicable
  • [ ] Full material datasheet requested (Tg, Td, T260/T288) for thermally demanding boards
  • [ ] Grade choice reviewed with the fabricator before the stackup is frozen
