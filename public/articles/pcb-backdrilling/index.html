PCB Backdrilling: How Removing Via Stubs Improves High-Speed Signal Integrity

Backdrilling removes the unused stub of a plated via to clean up high-speed signals. How it works, when to specify it, and the rules that keep it buildable.

Published November 15, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

A through-hole via rarely connects all the layers it passes through. A signal might use only the top four layers, but the plated barrel continues to layer 12 — a dead length of copper that goes nowhere. That leftover section is the via stub, and on high-speed boards it is far from harmless.

Backdrilling removes the stub: a slightly larger drill re-enters the hole from the back side and cuts away the unused barrel.

Why stubs hurt high-speed signals

A stub is an unterminated transmission line hanging off the signal path: a fast edge hits the end of the stub, reflects, and comes back to interfere with the signal.

The damage peaks at the stub's quarter-wave resonance, set by stub length and the laminate's dielectric constant: f = c / (4 × L × √εr). For FR-4 (dielectric constant near 4), a 2.5 mm stub resonates around 15 GHz; a 1.0 mm stub pushes that to roughly 37 GHz. The stub also adds capacitive loading and degrades return loss below resonance — on a multi-gigabit link, that means a notch in insertion loss and a closed eye.

The longer the stub, the lower the frequency at which the damage starts — which is why the problem grows with board thickness. A finished thickness of 0.15–7.0 mm covers a wide range, and the stub problem is worst at the thick end.

What backdrilling actually is

Backdrilling is a controlled-depth drilling step done after the via is plated. A slightly larger bit re-enters the via from the side opposite the signal layers and removes the unused barrel, stopping just past the signal layer.

The key word is controlled depth: the drill must stop close to — but never through — the layer carrying the signal. The barrel left behind is the remaining stub. Industry practice commonly targets a remaining stub of 0.5 mm or less, with tighter targets on the fastest links. The exact number depends on data rate, laminate, and link-budget margin.

Because the backdrill bit is larger than the original hole, it also removes the annular ring and plating on the layers it passes through — expected, since those layers are not connected to the via. What matters is that the drill does not cut into a plane or trace on a layer the via is meant to connect to.

When backdrilling earns its place

Backdrilling is not a default step for every board. It earns its place where stubs are long and signals are fast:

  • Multi-gigabit serial links. PCIe, Ethernet, USB and SerDes at several gigabits per second are the classic case — at these rates a millimetre-scale stub sits squarely in the signal bandwidth.
  • Thick boards with shallow signals. On a 12-layer or thicker board, high-speed signals on the outer layers leave long stubs behind every through via.
  • Dense BGA fan-out. Fine-pitch BGAs force signals through vias spanning the full stackup. Backdrilling lets the designer keep cheaper through vias while controlling the stub.
  • High-frequency RF boards. PTFE laminates are chosen for low loss at high frequencies, and a stub degrades the same low-loss path the material was bought for.

Do not specify backdrilling where the signals are slow. Below a few hundred megahertz, stubs are electrically short and harmless — backdrilling would add cost for no measurable benefit.

Design rules that keep it buildable

Specify the remaining stub, not just "backdrill". The drawing should state the maximum remaining stub allowed and which vias it applies to. "Backdrill all high-speed vias to within 0.5 mm of the signal layer" is a specification; "backdrill" alone is a suggestion every shop will interpret differently.

Say which vias get backdrilled. Mark them in the design data — usually the vias on high-speed nets. Backdrilling every via wastes time and adds risk on vias that do not need it, such as power and ground stitching vias.

Mind the drill sizes. The backdrill bit is larger than the original hole, so the clearance around the via must accommodate it. With a 0.15 mm minimum mechanical drill and 3/3 mil trace and space as the baseline, the layout still needs keep-out around each backdrilled via for the larger bit plus tolerance.

Watch the layer the drill stops at. The drill stops just past the deepest connected layer — in a 1–30 layer stackup with a deep signal layer, the backdrill passes through many layers of copper, each one a chance to nick a plane if depth control is off.

Mind the copper weight. Heavy copper complicates backdrilling: thick copper (up to 8 oz outer, 4 oz inner) is harder to drill cleanly and leaves more burr.

Material grade matters. Standard FR-4 grades TG135, TG150 and TG170 cover most backdrilling work. PTFE laminates drill differently — softer, more prone to smear — so parameters must match the material.

Alternatives when backdrilling is not enough

Backdrilling is one tool for stub control, not the only one:

  • Blind and buried vias. A blind via spanning only the layers it connects has no stub at all. They cost more and add lamination steps, but they eliminate the problem at the source.
  • Microvias. Laser-drilled microvias, typically 0.10 mm in diameter, connect only adjacent layers. A stack of microvias replaces a through via entirely on dense HDI boards — no stub, no backdrill.
  • Routing on the right layers. Sometimes the cheapest fix is layout: route the high-speed signal on a layer close to the side the via enters, so the stub is short even without backdrilling.

The right choice depends on layer count, data rate and budget — settle it in a DFM review before fabrication, because changing via structures after the stackup is frozen is expensive.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is specifying backdrilling without a remaining-stub number: the shop picks its own default, which may be looser than the link budget allows.

The second is backdrilling through the signal layer: a depth error that cuts into the signal layer severs the connection and the board fails open. The drawing must show exactly which layer each backdrilled via connects to.

The third is forgetting that backdrilling is a mechanical step with tolerances of its own. Drill wander, depth variation and registration error all eat into the remaining-stub budget — leave margin for the process, not just for the signal.

The fourth is specifying backdrilling on vias that do not need it. Every backdrilled via is an extra drilling operation and an extra inspection — reserve it for the nets whose signal integrity actually depends on it.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] High-speed nets identified; backdrilled vias marked
  • [ ] Maximum remaining stub specified (e.g. ≤ 0.5 mm)
  • [ ] Signal layer for each backdrilled via documented
  • [ ] Clearance around backdrilled vias sized for the larger bit
  • [ ] Thickness and layers confirmed (1–30 layers; 0.15–7.0 mm)
  • [ ] Copper weights agreed (up to 4 oz inner / 8 oz outer)
  • [ ] Material grade chosen (TG135/150/170 FR-4, or PTFE)
  • [ ] Alternatives considered: blind/buried vias, microvias, re-routing
  • [ ] Depth-control margin included in the stub budget
  • [ ] Backdrill excluded from low-speed and power/ground nets
