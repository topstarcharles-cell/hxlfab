Aluminum-Core PCB Thermal Design: How Metal-Core Boards Move Heat

Aluminum-core PCB thermal design: how metal-core boards move heat from LEDs and power devices, dielectric choices, layout rules, and pre-order checks.

Published November 18, 2026

Get a free DFM review ↗Start an RFQ →

A high-power LED or a switching MOSFET rarely fails because the circuit is wrong. It fails because the heat has nowhere to go. On a standard FR-4 board, heat must travel through epoxy and glass weave — a material with a thermal conductivity around 0.3 W/m·K, roughly a thousand times worse than copper. That is why high-power boards run hot and LEDs dim and shift colour.

An aluminum-core PCB — also called a metal-core PCB or MCPCB — changes the heat path. A sheet of aluminum sits under the circuit layer, bonded through a thin thermally conductive dielectric. Heat crosses the dielectric, spreads through the aluminum, and leaves through a heatsink or the enclosure.

How the heat actually travels

Think of the thermal path as a chain of resistances, in °C/W: junction, solder joint, copper layer, dielectric, aluminum base, then ambient air or a heatsink. Total temperature rise is power dissipated times the sum of those resistances — and the chain is only as good as its worst link.

On a metal-core board, the worst link is almost always the dielectric. It is thin — typically 75 to 150 µm — but its thermal conductivity is modest, commonly 1 to 3 W/m·K for standard thermally conductive dielectrics, with higher-performance grades reaching further at higher cost. A dielectric twice as thick roughly doubles that part of the resistance; one with twice the conductivity roughly halves it.

The aluminum base is rarely the bottleneck: common alloys conduct heat at roughly 130 to 200 W/m·K, and the base is thick — 1.0 to 3.0 mm is typical — so heat spreads laterally with ease once it arrives. The base also doubles as a mechanical stiffener and mounting surface.

Choosing the dielectric: the central trade-off

The dielectric has two jobs that pull in opposite directions: conduct heat, and insulate electrically. Thinner and more conductive moves heat better; thicker gives higher breakdown voltage. The choice follows the application:

  • LED lighting, low-voltage DC. The classic metal-core application: modest voltages, so a thin, conductive dielectric is usually right, and the board's main job is keeping the LED junction cool.
  • Mains-powered or high-voltage circuits. The dielectric is also a safety barrier: breakdown voltage and creepage and clearance rules take priority over thermal performance.
  • Power converters and motor drives. Currents are high and switching noise is a concern. A thicker copper circuit layer — 2 oz or more — spreads heat laterally before it crosses the dielectric, and high-current loops must stay tight.

Specify the dielectric by its datasheet values, not by a marketing label: thermal conductivity in W/m·K, thermal resistance for a stated thickness, and breakdown voltage. Two dielectrics both sold as "thermally conductive" can differ two- or threefold in conductivity — and that shows up directly as junction temperature.

Construction options: single-sided, double-sided, multilayer

Most metal-core boards are single-sided: one copper circuit layer on the dielectric, on the aluminum base — the simplest and cheapest construction, suited to LED arrays and power stages where one routing layer is enough.

Double-sided metal-core boards add a second circuit layer on the other side of the aluminum, but the metal core cannot be drilled through like FR-4 — a plated hole through the aluminum would short the layers together. Practical designs use insulated vias, holes lined with an insulating sleeve before plating, or avoid through-connections entirely. Both add cost; agree the approach with the fabricator early.

Multilayer metal-core boards are a specialist construction, with the aluminum core in the middle of the stackup and the same via-isolation problem at every layer transition. If the design genuinely needs four or more routing layers plus a metal core, confirm the construction, via scheme and cost in a DFM review before the layout is frozen — it is not a standard rigid multilayer build, and the published envelope for conventional rigid boards (1–30 layers, 0.15–7.0 mm) does not describe it.

Thermal vias and copper: helping the heat cross the dielectric

On FR-4, thermal vias move heat from the top layer to a ground plane or heatsink. On a metal-core board their role is different: the aluminum base is already the heatsink, so the problem is getting heat across the dielectric, not down through the board.

A heavy copper circuit layer — 2 oz and above — spreads heat laterally from a small footprint over a larger dielectric area, so more of the dielectric carries the load. A large thermal pad under a power device, soldered with good coverage, does the same thing. A via landing on the aluminum core without insulation is a short, and a via array under a component is useless if the solder voids beneath it.

Mounting screws are an underrated part of the thermal design: a board bolted tightly to a heatsink transfers heat across the whole back face, while one held by two screws at one end leaves an air gap no dielectric can compensate for. Keep the mounting pattern even and the back face flat.

LED-specific design points

LEDs make the thermal problem visible: light output falls and colour shifts as junction temperature rises, and lifetime roughly halves for every 10 °C of sustained increase. The design target is therefore a junction temperature, not a board temperature.

Work backwards from the LED datasheet: maximum junction temperature minus ambient and heatsink-to-ambient rise leaves the budget for the board itself. If it does not close, the options are a thinner or more conductive dielectric, heavier copper, more board area under the LED, or a better heatsink — in that order of cost. Keep LED strings thermally symmetric — equal copper, equal spacing, no LED over a cutout — or the hotter LEDs dim first and the luminaire looks uneven.

When a metal core is the wrong answer

A metal-core board is a thermal solution, not a general-purpose upgrade. Do not specify one where the heat does not justify it:

  • Low-power signal boards. A sensor or control board dissipating milliwatts gains nothing from an aluminum base and pays for it in cost, weight and routing constraints.
  • Dense multilayer signal designs. If the design needs four or more routing layers with fine features — 3/3 mil trace and space, 0.10 mm laser vias, 0.15 mm mechanical drills — a conventional rigid multilayer board is the right construction. A metal core in the middle complicates every via without helping signal integrity.
  • Boards that need thermal isolation, not conduction. A precision analog front end next to a power stage may need the two thermally separated, not coupled through a shared aluminum plate.

Where the heat is real but the routing is dense, heavy copper on FR-4 is the alternative: up to 4 oz inner-layer and 8 oz outer-layer copper spreads heat within the board without a metal core. The choice belongs in the DFM review, with the actual power dissipation on the table.

Common mistakes

Specifying the dielectric by brand name alone. "Thermally conductive dielectric" is not a specification. Name the conductivity, thickness and breakdown voltage.

Forgetting the dielectric is the bottleneck. A thicker aluminum base does almost nothing if the dielectric is thick and low-conductivity.

Routing a via through the metal core. A plated hole through aluminum shorts the circuit unless it is insulated first — and insulated vias cost more and need planning, not discovery at quoting.

Solder voids under thermal pads. A voided thermal pad is an air gap where the heat must cross. Stencil design and reflow profile matter as much as the board.

Ignoring the mechanical interface. A warped board or a single mounting screw leaves the aluminum base thermally disconnected from its heatsink.

Specifying metal core "to be safe". On a low-power board the premium buys nothing, and the routing and weight penalties stay.

Pre-order checklist

  • [ ] Power dissipation per heat source and the junction-temperature budget from the datasheet
  • [ ] Dielectric specified by conductivity (W/m·K), thickness and breakdown voltage — not by marketing label
  • [ ] Copper weight chosen to spread heat laterally under each heat source (2 oz or more for power stages)
  • [ ] Thermal pad design and stencil checked for voiding risk under power devices and LEDs
  • [ ] Single-sided, double-sided or multilayer metal-core construction confirmed with the fabricator, including the via scheme
  • [ ] Mounting pattern even across the board; back-face flatness and thermal interface material defined
  • [ ] LED strings thermally symmetric: equal copper, equal spacing, no broken heat paths
  • [ ] Heavy-copper FR-4 considered and ruled out (or chosen) with a reason, not by default
  • [ ] Board outline, finished thickness and panel size within the fabrication envelope (up to 850 × 520 mm panel)
  • [ ] Construction, dielectric and via scheme reviewed in a DFM review before the layout is frozen
